# A Thought Is Not a Fact

At 2 a.m., a thought arrives that feels like the single most obvious truth you've ever encountered. *You're going to lose everything. Nobody actually likes you. You've wasted it, all of it, and it's too late now.* It doesn't knock. It doesn't present evidence. It just arrives, fully formed, wearing the exact same certainty as the thought that told you, an hour earlier, to remember to buy milk.

That's the part almost nobody notices in the moment: the mind doesn't sort its own output by accuracy before handing it to you. It manufactures thoughts constantly, all night and all day, true ones and false ones and cruel ones and outdated ones, and it hands every single one over with identical confidence, identical font size, identical insistence that this one, unlike all the others, deserves to be believed.

Buddhist teaching has a phrase for the way out of this that keeps getting rediscovered independently in completely different rooms: you are the sky, not the weather passing through it. A storm can be violent, can genuinely darken everything, and still be nothing more than a temporary condition moving across something that was never actually threatened by it. The Stoics arrived at nearly the same place from a different direction — Epictetus taught that an impression arrives on its own, uninvited, but believing it is a separate act, one you're free to withhold. Modern cognitive therapy arrived at the same insight decades later, in clinics, with none of the philosophy, simply by noticing that the thoughts causing people the most suffering were reliably the least accurate ones in the room.

Three completely different starting points. Same discovery, every time: a thought showing up is not the same event as a thought being true.

None of this makes the 2 a.m. thought weaker in the moment. It still arrives at full volume. But there's a difference between a storm you're standing inside of, certain it will never end, and a storm you're watching pass across a sky that was there before it and will be there after. You don't have to argue with the thought, or defeat it, or even believe you're strong enough to withstand it. You only have to remember, however faintly, that you're the sky it's currently crossing — not the weather itself.
