# What You Feed, Grows

An old Cherokee grandfather tells his grandson that inside every person, two wolves are fighting. One is anger, envy, resentment, self-pity, grudges held long past their use. The other is joy, patience, humility, kindness, love. The boy thinks about it and asks which wolf wins. The grandfather answers: the one you feed.

The reason that story has survived this long isn't the wolves. It's the mechanism underneath them, and the mechanism is exactly as true for things that have nothing to do with good and evil. Attention isn't a neutral spotlight that illuminates things without touching them. Attention is more like water, or fuel, or money — a finite resource, and wherever you spend it, something on the receiving end gets stronger. This is true of a grudge. It's also true of a skill, a friendship, a fear, a business idea, a wound from twenty years ago, and a talent nobody's noticed yet, including you.

Look at it from any angle and the same shape appears. A muscle grows because you keep putting stress on it, not because you wanted it to grow in the abstract. A language gets fluent because you kept returning to it, not because you were talented at the start. And a resentment gets larger and more central to your identity for the same reason a muscle grows — you keep coming back and working it, usually without noticing you're doing it, usually while telling yourself you're just thinking something over one more time. There's no version of "I keep replaying this" that leaves the thing being replayed the same size it started at. It gets bigger. It has to. That's what feeding does.

The Stoics built an entire discipline around this, centuries before anyone had the language of neuroscience to explain why it works. Epictetus taught that the whole of a person's power lies in what they choose to give their attention to — not because attention is magic, but because it's the one thing genuinely within your control when almost nothing else is. Marcus Aurelius, writing to himself in what became the Meditations, kept returning to a version of the same instruction: guard your mind the way you'd guard a border, because whatever you let across it starts setting up residence. Buddhist teaching arrives at the same place from a completely different direction, describing the mind as a kind of field where whatever gets tended — a seed of anger, a seed of compassion — is what comes up. Three traditions, no contact with each other, same discovery: you don't get to opt out of feeding something. You only get to choose what.

This is a harder truth than it first sounds, because most of what people feed, they feed by accident. Nobody sits down and decides to grow their anxiety about money. They just think about it one more time before bed, and then again in the shower, and then again in traffic — each time telling themselves it's just problem-solving, just being responsible, just checking on it. And each time, whether or not any actual solving happens, the thing gets a little more watered. Meanwhile the friendship that could have deepened, the idea that could have gone somewhere, the gratitude that was sitting right there available — those went unfed simply because nothing was actively pulling attention toward them, and unfed things don't grow. They don't have to be killed. They just have to be ignored.

None of this requires never noticing the hard thing. Noticing isn't the same as feeding — you can see a wolf without opening the gate. The difference is whether you keep returning, keep rehearsing, keep giving it the best material you have. What you feed, grows. It was never asking you to be perfect about which wolf shows up. It was only ever asking which one you keep putting food in front of.
